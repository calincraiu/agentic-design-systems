# An LLM continues text with what's likely.

> Out loud, together: **"Form follows …"** and **"Less is …"**
>
> Now: **who wrote "less is more" first?**

<!-- notes -->
**The idea.** An **LLM** (large language model) is the AI inside Claude. It learned from an enormous amount of text, and what it does is continue text: given everything written so far, it writes a likely next piece, then the next, one small piece at a time. It picks among the likely options with a little randomness, which is why the same question can get slightly different answers.

You just did the same thing. Nobody had to *think* to finish "form follows function" or "less is more": you've seen those words together so often that the ending arrives by itself. Most people then say Mies van der Rohe wrote "less is more". He made it famous, but the poet Robert Browning wrote it in 1855, in *Andrea del Sarto*. The likely answer and the true answer were different, and the wrong one felt just as certain as the right ones.

**Why it matters.** Most of the time, likely and true are the same, which is why LLMs are so useful. But the answer comes out the same way, with the same confidence, either way. So the question is never "does it sound sure?" but "where did this come from, and how could I check it?" The rest of Part 1 is about the parts you add so that you *can* check.

**Try it yourself.** In Chat, ask for a detail you know well about a building you know well. Then ask for something more obscure about it, such as the exact height of a room. Notice that the tone doesn't change.
