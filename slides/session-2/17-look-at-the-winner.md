# Look at the winner

<p class="lead">Is it <em>calm, but noticed from the river</em>?</p>

> "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Marilyn Strathern, 1997, after Charles Goodhart

<!-- notes -->
The winning tower did what the rules asked: the most floor area while keeping the park's sun loss under 20%. Hold it up against the original wish. Nothing in the rules said *calm*, and nothing measured how it looks from the river. Whatever the measure doesn't see, the search is free to sacrifice.

This is **Goodhart's law**, named after the economist Charles Goodhart (1975). The wording on the slide is the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern's, from *'Improving ratings': audit in the British University system* (European Review, 1997). A measure that describes a design well stops describing it well once something searches for the best score. An agent searches harder and faster than a person, so it finds the gaps sooner.

Two fixes are open to you, and they're the next step: add measures for what the winner traded away, and set limits that say what you won't accept. Neither removes the problem. Each measure you add has its own blind side.

Write down one thing the winning tower got away with that a measure could catch, and one that no measure could.
